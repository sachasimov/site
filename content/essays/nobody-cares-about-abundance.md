---
title: Nobody Cares About Abundance
date: 2026-11-24
---

Every AI lab says the same thing. AI will cure cancer, make energy almost free, compress a century of science into ten years. Abundance for everyone.

I don't buy it. Not that they're lying on purpose. Most of them believe it. But it's not why they do it.

These people already have everything. The median engineer at Anthropic and OpenAI makes $700,000 a year. And since Anthropic's IPO last week, its average employee is worth around $44 million on paper, with at least 400 early hires at $100 million or more ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-millionaire-employees-billionaire-early-hires-wealth-concentration-warning-dario-amodei/)).

That's already a lot of abundance, and we have yet to read about the new wave of philanthropy coming out of these fortunes.

What they want is to understand what intelligence is, to see what happens when you build it, and to be the one who builds it.

I know this because I'm one of them (although with much less money).

## Listen to how they talk about themselves

Easy test: forget what they say about AI. Listen to what they say about themselves.

Demis Hassabis never hid it. He's been saying for years that the point is to understand the universe, and AI is the tool. To his biographer Sebastian Mallaby: "Doing science is like reading the mind of God. Understanding the deep mystery of the universe is my religion."

Mallaby also tells this story: during his PhD, Hassabis read *Ender's Game*, a novel about a kid genius put in charge of saving humanity, and felt someone had finally written a book about him.

AlphaFold will save lives and he's happy about it. But it's a by-product. It's not what he's after.

Musk is even more direct. At the Grok 4 launch he asked whether AI would be good or bad for humanity. Probably good, he said. Then: "But I somewhat reconciled myself to the fact that even if it wasn't going to be good, I'd at least like to be alive to see it happen."

He's been saying this for ten years. In 2014 he tweeted that he hoped we weren't "just the biological boot loader for digital superintelligence", and that it was "increasingly probable". More recently: "I want to know what is real, even if the answer is total obliteration of my consciousness." xAI's mission is to "understand the true nature of the universe".

In other words, he's ok with the risk of extinction as long as he gets to see how it plays out.

The others are quieter, but it's there. Ilya Sutskever got into AI because "I was simply curious about how intelligence works". AGI was "the mountain to climb".

Sam Altman is harder to read. He calls himself "an AI nerd since I was a kid" and writes long essays about intelligence becoming "wildly abundant". Maybe he means both.

Geoffrey Hinton, asked how he could build something he now thinks is dangerous, said "the prospect of discovery is too sweet". He was borrowing from Oppenheimer: "When you see something that is technically sweet, you go ahead and do it, and you argue about what to do about it only after you have had your technical success."

None of this is hidden. It's all in podcasts, biographies and livestreams.

## Not all of them

To be fair, not everyone is like this. Dario Amodei has said his father's death pushed him from physics into biology, and *Machines of Loving Grace* is the most serious attempt by a lab leader to lay out what AI could do for medicine. Mustafa Suleyman has spent years going after labs that treat superintelligence as "inevitable and even desirable", and he names Musk.

So it's not everyone at the top. You won't find this with Dario or Mustafa. But talk to researchers at Anthropic or DeepMind and a lot of them will tell you the same thing: they want to see what happens, even if it goes badly.

## Why abundance doesn't work on people who already have it

None of this makes them villains. Abundance is simply a weak motivator for people who already live in it, and the people building frontier AI live in a great deal of it.

Including me. I work in AI, I make a lot of money, and I live very well.

Flying cars wouldn't change my life much.

I'm not sure I want to live to 150.

I've never experienced the death of someone really close to me, so I don't obsess over sickness.

I have very little faith in our ability as a society to share the gains of AI. I put more probability on a future where everyone is plugged into virtual worlds like in Ready Player One, or one where AI and robots produce abundance for a ruling class of a few hundred million people while everyone else is left to die, than on one where everyone sings and dances in communist bliss.

I do this because I want to know what the hell we're doing here. What's out there. What intelligence, consciousness and life are, and why on earth there's a universe in the first place.

And to be really frank:

If this work carries a real chance of catastrophe, a curious person has to find some way to live with that. I can't carry the possibility of human extinction around and still do my job, so I put it down and go back to the problem in front of me.

In a sense I understand Musk's point: I don't believe I have the agency to stop the march of the world, so I'd rather enjoy the view.

I'd bet most of my colleagues do the same.

Abundance is a useful story for that: it lets us explain the work to everyone else, and occasionally to ourselves.

But abundance is a lie, or at least a hope that it comes as a by-product.
